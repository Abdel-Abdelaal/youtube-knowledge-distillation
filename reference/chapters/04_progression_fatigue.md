# 4. Progression, fatigue & recovery

This chapter covers how a lift, a program and a lifter move forward over months and years (progression), how he manages the fatigue that training creates without deload weeks (fatigue, deloads, breaks), and what happens outside the gym (sleep and recovery). His stance: progressive overload is mandatory for naturals, best done through evolving rep ranges with small, steady, never-stopping progress and stable tonnage. He rejects scheduled deloads, blocks and peaking for bodybuilders and builds fatigue management into every week instead. Sleep and passive rest are the base of recovery; soreness and "feel" are poor guides.

## Why progression matters

He treats rising tonnage as the target and getting stronger as the main way to raise it. Rep ranges and intensity windows themselves are in ch. 3.

## Overload levers and their order

Load, reps, sets and frequency are all overload, but he insists the bar weight never leaves the mix.

### PRG-11 · Overload has many modes
- **Statement:** Overload can come from load, reps, sets, frequency, rest, technique/variation or range of motion; weight on the bar is just "the tip of the iceberg", and more sets and reps at the same weight are valid overload.
- **Numbers:** —
- **Scope:** All trainees.
- **Confidence:** high — ~8 videos, 2020→2023
- **Status:** current
- **Sources:** {{xn8RN9807Ns@318}} {{8ELQ2XZgpls@638}} {{yUecX6qONXg@1015}} {{7uBT8vp0lNw@882}}

### PRG-12 · Bar weight must stay in the mix
- **Statement:** Weight on the bar is the simplest, most reliable and easiest lever to log and must stay in the mix, but load-only progression is "designed to fail" because the ability to add weight outpaces recovery (the "Milo paradox").
- **Numbers:** —
- **Scope:** All trainees past the novice stage.
- **Confidence:** high — ~10 videos, 2020→2024
- **Status:** changed — 2020-21: bar weight is the simplest and best method; 2022: weight is the "tip of the iceberg", most natural gains come from accumulated volume; 2024: his own bar-weight obsession caused progression anxiety, yet you must still get stronger.
- **Check:** Does the program raise load as well as reps or sets, without relying on load alone?
- **Sources:** {{ML5AQa9THHI@63}} {{l9iNYJtRrD8@187}} {{YIIWlqhoNA0@1009}} {{16fjHEtZTCA@1209}}

### PRG-13 · Reps-only or sets-only progression stalls
- **Statement:** Adding only reps or only sets at a fixed weight also stalls: the weight becomes too easy, reps drift into low-quality or cardio-limited volume, and sets become junk volume. You need both load and volume.
- **Numbers:** Example drift: 12, 13, 14, 15 reps at 225.
- **Scope:** All trainees.
- **Confidence:** high — ~8 videos, 2020→2023
- **Status:** current
- **Sources:** {{RqS8AxVE28I@251}} {{l9iNYJtRrD8@311}} {{9y2s6kyFkPc@1941}} {{h3U30oRVrMU@1638}}

### PRG-14 · Order of the levers
- **Statement:** Step loading adds stimulus as reps first (evolving range), then sets, then frequency, and weight last, always in small steps; elsewhere he ranks importance as weight, then reps, then sets, then frequency, with drop sets, TUT and pre-exhaustion only "the cherry on top".
- **Numbers:** Superset isolation volume grows 2 → 3-4 sets, spreads to other days, only then add weight.
- **Scope:** All trainees.
- **Confidence:** medium — ~4 videos, 2020→2022
- **Status:** contradicted — both orders stated in Dec 2022; the first probably ranks importance and the second gives the order of steps *[inference]*.
- **Sources:** {{1dMQdarQuHw@438}} {{LNggSj0WBd8@1887}} {{z2uzpl_smSg@1195}} {{0pKnDucWcDc@1761}}

- **Statement:** Volume and frequency are pulled when weight progress stops being realistic; intensity rises automatically as load progresses. When daily volume and intensity both stall, frequency is the lever left.
- **Numbers:** —

## Evolving rep ranges (double progression)

His main tool: each lift gets a fixed range, reps climb week to week, and weight goes up when the first set reaches the ceiling. Ch. 3 (INT-46) covers why declining reps across sets matter for intensity.

### PRG-20 · Evolving rep ranges are the core method
- **Statement:** Give each lift a fixed rep range, start near the bottom, add reps weekly, add weight at the top and stay in the same range. He calls it the best way to progress.
- **Numbers:** 6-10 example: 6,5 → … → 10,9,8 → jump; ~90-95% of his lifts (2020); "every one of his programs" (2024).
- **Scope:** All lifts past the novice stage (see PRG-36 for novices).
- **Confidence:** high — ~15 videos, 2020→2025
- **Status:** current
- **Check:** Does each lift have a rep range with a rule for when to add weight?
- **Sources:** {{rucwL-SIefg@314}} {{QfHfxLYRu88@2765}} {{d1eLAd5WvIA@3817}} {{ZLheQPgEfoA@504}}

### PRG-21 · Add weight when the first set hits the ceiling
- **Statement:** Only the first (top) set needs to reach the top of the range, with the others close; waiting for every set to hit the top turns the range back into a static scheme.
- **Numbers:** 6-10×4: 10-10-10-10 = should have jumped long ago, 10-9-8-7 = ready; 5-10: 8-7-7-5 → 9-8-7-6 → 10 → +5 lb.
- **Scope:** All lifts on evolving ranges.
- **Confidence:** high — ~8 videos, 2020→2024
- **Status:** changed — Jan 2020: on 4×12, go up only if all four sets of 12 are comfortable; from Aug 2020: waiting for all sets makes ranges static; 2024: one set at the top is enough.
- **Check:** Is weight added once the first set reaches the top of the range, not only when every set does?
- **Sources:** {{xFVva4-swKc@188}} {{CrT1WOhxLqU@64}} {{CrT1WOhxLqU@190}} {{ZLheQPgEfoA@566}}

### PRG-22 · Readiness depends on the range
- **Statement:** On a heavy low range less is needed to add weight; you needn't max every set.
- **Numbers:** On 3-5, 5,4 is enough; on an 8-12 isolation, 11,10; 5-4 or 5-4-3 can be enough.
- **Scope:** All lifts on evolving ranges.
- **Confidence:** medium — 2 videos, 2021→2022
- **Status:** current
- **Sources:** {{X8WMlqP6M78@308}} {{QfHfxLYRu88@2640}}

### PRG-23 · Reps should fall across sets
- **Statement:** At the same weight, reps should drop from set to set; identical reps on three or more sets mean sandbagging or too light a load.
- **Numbers:** E.g. 12 → 7; 10, 9-8, 8-7; 4×10 repeated after 3 min rest = add weight; 7,7,7 → 8,7,7 is fine if the top set progresses.
- **Scope:** All working sets.
- **Confidence:** high — ~10 videos, 2020→2024
- **Status:** current
- **Sources:** {{CrT1WOhxLqU@127}} {{LYBdXCcmOO0@691}} {{P89o8FBSWDI@997}} {{okm6bZHKHI4@4888}}

### PRG-24 · Straight sets, no pyramids
- **Statement:** Use the same weight on all working sets; pyramids and reverse pyramids ("D tier") pile up junk volume.
- **Numbers:** —
- **Scope:** All working sets.
- **Confidence:** high — ~5 videos, 2020→2023
- **Status:** current
- **Sources:** {{LYBdXCcmOO0@502}} {{_2M6FhWuIvs@220}} {{o_x3Ij2GBXM@120}} {{SxqjF2oPasg@692}}

### PRG-25 · Every set stays inside the range
- **Statement:** If a set falls below the floor after a jump, drop the next set and win it back next session; if the first set misses, return to the previous weight. Falling below means the jump was too big or the range was badly set ("too greedy").
- **Numbers:** E.g. 7 then 6 on 6-10 after a jump.
- **Scope:** All lifts on evolving ranges.
- **Confidence:** high — ~6 videos, 2020→2023
- **Status:** current
- **Sources:** {{CrT1WOhxLqU@376}} {{9y2s6kyFkPc@315}} {{43F9unmSc_I@1007}} {{1TKqwXCge9E@638}}

### PRG-26 · Evolving sets: add a set, then weight
- **Statement:** When the first set tops the range, add a set; at the top set count, add weight and drop back a set, then rebuild.
- **Numbers:** OHP 4×8-12 at 100 lb: at 12/11/10 add 2.5 lb and go to 3 sets; if 3 sets can't stay in range after a jump do 2 (e.g. 7, 6), then add the third back; 12 → 15-16 weekly sets, then add load and return to 12.
- **Scope:** Compounds and isolation, intermediates.
- **Confidence:** high — ~6 videos, 2022→2024
- **Status:** changed — 2020: he avoided evolving set ranges because removing both limits removes structure; 2021: advanced tools that destabilize programs; 2022-24: standard in his programs.
- **Sources:** {{LYBdXCcmOO0@940}} {{X8WMlqP6M78@720}} {{BhOuuyriBDw@1383}} {{c7hl9u3h_s0@5990}}

### PRG-27 · Set ranges one set wide
- **Statement:** Keep set ranges one set wide; wide set ranges stall progress, especially on compounds. 3-4 sets beats a fixed 4 because you can try a heavier weight without "failing".
- **Numbers:** 2-3 or 3-4 sets, not 1-4.
- **Scope:** Mainly compounds.

## Range width

### PRG-37 · Ranges that are too narrow fail
- **Statement:** Narrow ranges leave no room for set evolution and drift toward sandbagged 5x5; keep at least 4 reps between floor and ceiling.
- **Numbers:** Too narrow: 5-6, 6-8, 8-10 ×4 sets, 10-12 ×2-3 sets, 4×8-10 squat; good: 6-10, 8-12; 4-6 "a recipe for plateaus" (2025).
- **Scope:** All lifts.
- **Confidence:** high — ~10 videos, 2020→2025
- **Status:** changed — 2020 he often prescribed 4-6 (4×4-6 pull-ups/dips, 3×4-6 squat); 2023-25: ≥4 reps between floor and ceiling, 4-6 plateaus.
- **Sources:** {{0yIieHQjEiA@64}} {{87CMjYjX2jY@125}} {{1TKqwXCge9E@638}} {{ACFKXT44BzM@498}}

### PRG-38 · Ranges that are too wide fail
- **Statement:** Very wide ranges (top more than 2× the bottom) take forever to earn a jump and invite laziness; 6-12 is about the widest he allows (2024).
- **Numbers:** Too wide: 2-20, 2-12, 3×3-20, 8-16, 8-18; 10-20 only with 4 sets; replace 8-20 with 10-15 or 14-20.
- **Scope:** All lifts.
- **Confidence:** high — ~8 videos, 2020→2024
- **Status:** contradicted — 8-15: too wide because reaching 15 means you'll get 12-13 (2020) vs hitting 15 lets you add ~10 lb and still get 8 (2023).
- **Sources:** {{0O0wnhU3g-8@129}} {{sCMGXCXKVjs@252}} {{1TKqwXCge9E@574}} {{c7hl9u3h_s0@3261}}

### PRG-39 · Wide ranges for lifts where reps fall fast
- **Statement:** Use a wide range like 6-12 where reps drop fast across sets or the lift is hard to progress; incline and preacher curls plateau easily and need it.
- **Numbers:** 5×6-12 drops 12 → 6-7; 8 reps at 40 lb won't survive a jump to 4 reps.
- **Scope:** Spoto press, incline/preacher curls and similar lifts.
- **Confidence:** medium — ~5 videos, 2020→2025
- **Status:** current
- **Sources:** {{t_RZT-AkjhE@380}} {{xQQJUXf58ZE@1177}} {{DA7QvIAyptw@1326}} {{ACFKXT44BzM@434}}

### PRG-40 · Higher ranges: easy reps, hard jumps
- **Statement:** Wider or higher ranges make each added rep easier, but the higher the range, the harder it is to clear and add weight.
- **Numbers:** Bottom is 50% of top in 4-8, 60% in 6-10.
- **Scope:** All lifts.
- **Confidence:** medium — 2 videos, 2024→2025
- **Status:** current
- **Sources:** {{ACFKXT44BzM@498}} {{6p2x4kDDjzs@5730}}

### PRG-41 · Adjust the range to a plateau
- **Statement:** When stalled in a range, adjust it to the exercise: widen if low reps stall, shrink if high reps stall; try widening first so tonnage climbs at the same weight.
- **Numbers:** —
- **Scope:** Stalled lifts.
- **Confidence:** medium — 2 videos, 2021→2025
- **Status:** current
- **Sources:** {{C86TMVYGu0M@328}} {{ACFKXT44BzM@1250}}

### PRG-42 · When testing a range, start wide
- **Statement:** When beta-testing a new range, start wider and narrow it; shrinking is safer than expanding.

## Strength-work progression schemes

Fixed-weight schemes for the heavy opening lift, where reps and sets evolve before the load moves. Lift-specific programming is in ch. 7.

### PRG-43 · The "543" elevator scheme
- **Statement:** For heavy deadlifts and OHP, keep the weight and build sets and reps up a ladder, then add weight and restart at the bottom.
- **Numbers:** 1-3 sets of 2-5: 2; 3,2; 4,3,2; 5,4,3; then +10 lb and restart at 2. Deadlift variant 2-3-2-4-3-2-5-3-2; a tight 1, 2-1, 3-2-1 version moves slower but may allow 10 lb jumps.
- **Scope:** His 2020 deadlift and OHP.
- **Confidence:** medium — ~4 videos, 2020
- **Status:** changed — 2020 schemes of 2-5 reps; by 2022 compounds never go below 4 (see PRG-102).
- **Sources:** {{t_RZT-AkjhE@318}} {{4Dop4_sIWn4@64}} {{65rLZe9cz_0@314}} {{6lggo2XPomI@377}}

### PRG-44 · Fixed-weight squat and bench ladder
- **Statement:** For squat and bench, keep the weight and climb a rep ladder, then add weight and return to the start; a failed session is repeated.
- **Numbers:** 4 → 5,4 → 6,5,4 → 7,5,3 → 8,6,4, then +5 lb/side; 6-5-4 (15 reps) isn't enough to jump, 8-6-4 (18) is. Higher-volume version where form breaks at high intensity: 4; 5,4; 6,5,4; 7,6,5; 8,7,6.
- **Scope:** His squat and bench, 2020-21.
- **Confidence:** medium — ~4 videos, 2020→2021

## Rule-based alternatives he endorses

### PRG-48 · The 85% rule (step loading)
- **Statement:** Build reps and sets at a load until it feels like ~70% effort, then add weight to get back to ~85%, cut sets or reps, and rebuild; never keep piling volume at 70%. Your 85% grows with you.
- **Numbers:** Close-grip bench 200 lb at 7/6/5 → build until the first set hits 10 and the last still gets 6 with a 4th set added → 10/9/8 at 200 → 205 lb gives ~7/6/6.
- **Scope:** General, arms included.
- **Confidence:** medium — ~3 videos, 2022→2023
- **Status:** current
- **Sources:** {{z2uzpl_smSg@1070}} {{z2uzpl_smSg@1259}} {{z2uzpl_smSg@1511}} {{HXoY7xzHlWU@1451}}

### PRG-49 · Rep-goal system
- **Statement:** Maxing every set at the same weight toward a total rep goal, then adding ~5 lb, is "excellent" and essentially evolving ranges by another name; he later prefers RIR for clearer brackets.

- **Scope:** General.
- **Confidence:** medium — ~2 videos, 2020→2021
- **Status:** changed — 2020: rep goals excellent; 2021: RIR preferred; 2023: day-chosen RIR autoregulation "S tier".

## Plateaus

### PRG-71 · What counts as a plateau
- **Statement:** Repeating a weight or reps for 2-3 sessions is the "breaking point" where gains happen, not a plateau; months at the same weight is one. Plateaus are normal and PRs every session are unrealistic (2023).
- **Numbers:** 2-3 sessions = normal; ~6 months = plateau; no lift should stagnate more than ~1 month vs 4-5 months on a compound means the program is wrong (both June 2020).
- **Scope:** All lifts.
- **Confidence:** medium — ~5 videos, 2020→2024
- **Status:** contradicted — stall tolerance of ~1 month vs 4-5 months, both June 2020.

### PRG-72 · Plateaus are a recovery or programming problem
- **Statement:** Plateaus come from poor recovery or diet, low effort, bad programming or a bad lift; in bodybuilding they usually mean too much volume to recover from or too little stimulus.
