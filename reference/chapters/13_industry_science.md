# 13. The fitness industry & reading science

This chapter covers how NH sees the fitness business (programs, coaching, supplements, influencers, YouTube itself), how to judge whom to trust, and how he weighs scientific evidence against lived experience. His stance: the industry exists to sell, hard work can't be sold, and lifting knowledge should be free, so learn to program for yourself and question anyone who profits from you. On science he calls himself experience-based rather than "science-based": he is against scientism and the "science-based" brand, not against the scientific method. Everything said here about named people and brands is his unverified opinion. PEDs and natural limits as such are in ch. 2, training variables in ch. 3-4, supplements in ch. 10.

## Selling knowledge: programs and coaching

He holds that knowledge you got for free should stay free, that a lifter who can program needs no one, and that most paid programs and coaching exploit beginners. His own paid tier from late 2024 is the main exception (see IND-21).

## Supplements, products and marketing

He sees supplements and fitness products as a sales machine that creates needs and fears, then sells the fix.

### IND-09 · The supplement industry exists to empty your wallet
- **Statement:** He describes the supplement industry as a conglomerate with one shared goal, your money, which has infiltrated every fitness sphere through sponsorships; ignore anyone who earns money or a cut from a supplement.
- **Numbers:** His figures: ~$35B/yr sales, ~3%/yr growth, ~$140B valuation (2021); US fitness industry ~$30B/yr (2022)
- **Scope:** All supplement advice from sponsored or selling creators
- **Confidence:** high — ~8 videos, 2020→2022
- **Status:** current

### IND-10 · The supplement marketing playbook
- **Statement:** Marketing creates a need, then plants suggestion ("you might not get enough protein"), then FOMO and fake scarcity; viral campaigns piggyback or sponsor memes, and the less an ad looks like an ad, the stronger it is. Understanding this makes you immune.
- **Numbers:** Ad potency: TV 1, YouTube ad 2, influencer 3, grassroots paid campaign 4; influencers act neutral, then launch their own product 3-4 months later; fake out-of-stock notices drive buying three tubs
- **Scope:** Consumers; he cites turkesterone and ecdysterone as sponsored virals
- **Confidence:** medium — 1 long video, 2021
- **Status:** current
- **Sources:** {{S_EF3ebs5Jg@952}} {{S_EF3ebs5Jg@1015}} {{S_EF3ebs5Jg@2657}} {{S_EF3ebs5Jg@3662}}

## Whom to trust

His filter: can the teacher demonstrate what he teaches, naturally, and does he have the character to be trusted? Fame, follower count and charisma prove nothing. Credentials are his third rule (SCI-20).

### IND-24 · Trust rules: demonstrate now, or prove it, and no lying
- **Statement:** Rule 1: can the person demonstrate now what they teach (big or strong now)? Rule 2: if not, do they have dated, verifiable proof they once did? A record of lying, hurting others or cowardice disqualifies. Asking a teacher for proof isn't egoistic; legendary coaches (Gironda, Simmons, Coan) proved it with their own bodies first. He prefers advice from people who look like they lift, while keeping critical thinking.
- **Numbers:** —
- **Scope:** Choosing any fitness teacher; rule 3 (credentials) is SCI-20
- **Confidence:** high — ~12 videos, 2020→2024
- **Status:** contradicted — "don't take hypertrophy advice from small people" vs "being bigger is not an argument" and "experience isn't how a physique looks" (SCI-22); he partly agrees you needn't be big to understand size, and reconciles via the halo effect (IND-26) and his natural-only rule (IND-25)
- **Check:** Can the person teaching hypertrophy show, now or with verifiable past proof, the result he teaches?
- **Sources:** {{4aIHcNEDTNA@0}} {{4aIHcNEDTNA@64}} {{fPU3nRIH8sM@6408}} {{GyK1Y6WTR-g@2976}}
